Research agenda

AI, law, and the institutions that put technology to work

My research focuses on AI and the law. I examine how AI systems affect established legal frameworks and how law can respond to the technical and institutional processes through which those systems are developed, evaluated, and deployed.

I want this work to help policymakers decide how to use and integrate AI into government so that government works better for the people it serves. That requires attention to both opportunity and accountability: how AI can support public work, where its use changes the grounds for legal decisions, and what information people and courts need to evaluate those decisions.

My approach looks beyond the model. Software scaffolding, computing infrastructure, evaluation practices, user interfaces, and institutional procedures all affect what an AI system can do and how its effects are distributed. These surrounding systems often reveal more observable points of control than model behavior alone, and can support legal responses that remain useful as models change.

AI, legal institutions, and regulatory governance

One strand of my work studies how technical systems relocate consequential choices across software development, infrastructure, and institutional decision-making. It asks how legal safeguards should respond when technical processes shape the evidence, capabilities, and disclosures on which public and private decisions depend.

AI-assisted administration and the administrative record

Shadow Judgment: AI and the Administrative Record examines agency use of systems that rank evidence, classify matters, or recommend action while a human official retains formal authority. Such tools can shape what the official sees and which options seem salient, even when their role is not fully reflected in the administrative record.

The project uses AI-assisted patent examination as a mechanism-tracing case study. It develops two complementary ideas: reasoned reliance, which asks agencies to explain when and why an AI-generated premise mattered to a decision; and functional incorporation, which asks agencies to include enough information about an AI-assisted process for courts to assess preserved claims of error or prejudice.

Patent disclosure and generative AI

Generative AI and the Limits of Patent Law asks whether patent disclosure can sustain the patent bargain when an invention’s value lies in a process that uses data, training methods, evaluation, and infrastructure to produce models with measurable capabilities. A fixed model may not capture the inventive contribution, while disclosure of every model weight or proprietary input may be unnecessary.

I develop a process- and performance-based approach to enablement centered on a reproducible training method and measurable performance characteristics. The project also examines how firms choose among patents, trade secrecy, contracts, and infrastructure control, and whether current disclosure rules are contributing to a shift toward secrecy.

Agentic AI and software scaffolding

Foundation models acquire the ability to browse, execute code, use private data, or act inside organizations through software scaffolding that connects them to tools, memory, and external systems. That software sets permissions, approval requirements, monitoring, and audit trails. This project asks whether governance duties should focus on those points where model-generated recommendations become real-world actions—for example, through access limits, human authorization for consequential acts, persistent logging, and rollback mechanisms.

Infrastructure and responsibility

My work on AI infrastructure asks where control, observability, and the capacity to reduce harm sit across the ecosystem. In Audio Deepfakes and the Regulation of the Landlords of Creativity, coauthored with George He, I examine the responsibilities of companies that own the computing resources and models used to train and deploy generative AI. This infrastructure-focused work extends to the other actors and processes surrounding AI systems.

Empirical legal studies

A second strand uses computational text analysis, machine learning, and natural language processing to study legal change and test questions that doctrinal analysis alone cannot answer. This work also examines the tools themselves: what they measure, when their results are reliable, and how methodological choices shape claims about law and AI.

Measuring legal influence

In Measuring Legal Importance: From Case Citations to Linguistic Shifts, I use computational text analysis to trace changes in patent language surrounding an influential Federal Circuit decision. The project illustrates how linguistic patterns can reveal legal impact that citation counts miss.

Evaluating what language models know

In the coauthored project Which Cases Do Language Models Already Know?, we test whether language models recognize cases before receiving their texts and whether that recognition can distort legal-AI evaluations. The project develops a screening protocol for researchers who use decided cases to assess language models or generate empirical measurements.

Methods for studying legal texts

With Michael A. Livermore, I have written about the growth of computational legal studies and the limits researchers should keep in view, including language coverage, domain fit, and the need to validate inputs and outputs.

Consultation and collaboration

With experience across software engineering, patent litigation, legal scholarship, and government, I bring a technically grounded perspective to legal and institutional questions raised by generative AI. I welcome conversations with law firms, lawyers, policymakers, journalists and news organizations, startups, and others exploring the implications of AI for their work. I am available as a subject-matter expert for consultation, speaking, and collaborative projects.
